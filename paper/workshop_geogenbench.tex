\begin{abstract}
Geometry benchmarks ask a model to read a diagram.
None asks it to draw one.
\GeoGenBench does.
It has 801 prompts, each asking for a plane-geometry construction and each paired with the checks a correct drawing must pass.
The model writes TikZ.
We compile it, recover the coordinates, and run every check exactly.
No human grades and no vision model judges.
We test six frontier models.
Asking for a typed intermediate representation instead of raw TikZ lifts every model, by 2 to 25 points, most for the weakest.
Under that scaffold the three best models tie within one point at a $7\times$ cost spread.
A human check of 200 outputs finds the grader lenient in one direction only: it never fails a correct drawing.
We release the dataset, the templates, the grader, and the leaderboard.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A language model asked to draw a geometry diagram will usually produce something that looks right.
Looking right is not the same as being right.
Every geometry benchmark so far tests reading: here is a diagram, answer a question.
None tests drawing, because grading a drawing has been the hard part.

Take the prompt \textit{``Draw an acute triangle $EFG$ with angles $60^\circ$ and $70^\circ$ at $E$ and $F$. Construct the altitude from $G$ meeting $EF$ at $H$.''}
A correct drawing has those two angles, a right angle at $H$, $H$ on the segment $EF$ and not beyond it, and no obtuse angle.
In our pilot, one flagship model put $H$ beyond the segment.
Another drew an obtuse triangle.
Both drawings looked fine.

Two ways to grade such a drawing exist today, and neither works.
A person can do it, slowly and inconsistently.
A vision model can say whether the picture looks like a right triangle, but not whether the angle is $90^\circ$.
We do a third thing.
We compile the model's drawing back into coordinates and test each requirement as arithmetic.
We call each requirement a \emph{check}, and a drawing that meets every check a \emph{pass}.
A right angle is then a calculation, not an opinion.

The rest of the paper follows one question after another.
\Cref{sec:design} says how the prompts and their checks are made and how a drawing is graded.
\Cref{sec:verification} asks whether the grader can be trusted, and tests it against adversarial drawings and against people.
\Cref{sec:results} grades six frontier models.
\Cref{sec:discussion} says what the numbers mean.

\paragraph{Prior work.}
Geometry3K~\citep{lu2021intergps}, GeoQA~\citep{chen2021geoqa}, UniGeo~\citep{chen2022unigeo}, MathVista~\citep{lu2024mathvista}, GeoEval~\citep{zhang2024geoeval}, and GeoBench~\citep{geobench2026} test diagram reading.
T2I-CompBench++~\citep{huang2025t2icompbenchpp} and GenExam~\citep{wang2025genexam} grade generated images with a model as judge.
AlphaGeometry~\citep{trinh2024alphageometry} and the Lean corpora machine-check proofs.
\GeoGenBench machine-checks drawings.

\section{How a drawing is graded}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Prompts and checks come from one template.}
A Python template writes both the sentence the model sees and the checks the grader runs (\cref{fig:cogen}).
The altitude template returns the prompt above and the list \texttt{[right\_angle($A$,$H$,$B$), point\_on\_segment($H$,$B$,$C$)]}.
Because both come from the same source, a check can never ask for something the prompt did not.
A reviewer worried about contamination can re-roll the template with a fresh seed.

\paragraph{From drawing to verdict.}
The model emits TikZ.
We compile it twice: once to an image, once to SymPy geometry with coordinates.
Fifteen checks, such as \propname{right\_angle}, \propname{midpoint}, \propname{parallel}, and \propname{point\_on\_segment}, run on those coordinates in microseconds, with a small numeric tolerance.
Two weaker checks on labels and marks count only toward a loose pass.
Template authors say what must hold, not how to test it.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
The model sees only the prompt.
An output passes if every check passes.
It gets a soft pass if nothing fails but a check was skipped.
Otherwise it fails, or it never rendered.

\paragraph{What is in the benchmark.}
600 of the 801 prompts come from 30 templates, 200 per tier: \tone is a single shape, \ttwo adds one construction, \tthree combines several.
The other 201 were extracted by a language model from a state K--12 curriculum~\citep{bluebonnet2024}.
We report the two sources separately and treat the templated 600 as the anchor.

\section{Can the grader be trusted}
\label{sec:verification}

\paragraph{Do the checks pin the drawing down.}
A check list is complete if every drawing that passes it is the intended construction, up to position and scale.
Of the 30 templates, 18 are that tight.
The other 12 pin the named relations but leave some parameters free, as a teacher's instructions usually do.

\paragraph{Can the checks be fooled.}
We tried to write TikZ that passes each check while contradicting the prompt.
Five checks can be fooled on their own.
Templates pair checks, so we estimate under 5\% of passes are exploitable.

\paragraph{Hardening.}
Three grader fixes promoted 4.6\% of pilot outputs from soft pass to pass and demoted none.
They also halved the apparent gap between the two strategies in \cref{sec:results}.
Part of that gap had been the grader's, not the models'.
Every number below is post-fix.

\paragraph{Does it agree with people.}
Two raters, a PhD mathematician and an engineer who has taught mathematics, judged 200 outputs blind to the grader's verdict.
Grader and raters agree moderately ($\kappa = 0.455$), below the 0.70 we had pre-registered, while the raters agree well with each other.
The disagreement runs one way.
The raters overturned 18\% of the grader's passes and none of its fails.
\Cref{sec:discussion} says what that means for the leaderboard.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Models and strategies.}
Six models from three vendors at a $20\times$ price spread: \opus, \sonnet, \haiku, \gpt, \geminipro, and \geminiflash.
Two strategies.
\rawcode hands the model the prompt and a TikZ tutorial and takes what it writes.
\structured asks for typed JSON describing points, segments, circles, and checks, which our code turns into SymPy and then TikZ.
The checks run before rendering, so the model gets up to three retries.
Every model-by-strategy cell runs on the 600 templated prompts three times (\cref{tab:headline}).

\paragraph{The scaffold lifts every model.}
\structured helps each model: \gpt by 2 points, \haiku by 5, \sonnet by 12, \opus by 22, and \geminipro by 25.
The weaker a model is at raw TikZ, the more it gains.

\paragraph{The frontier converges.}
With the scaffold, \gpt, \sonnet, and \opus finish at 94\%, 93\%, and 93\%, within one point of each other.
\opus costs seven times what \gpt does.
Without the scaffold the spread runs from 92\% down to 36\%, and \opus is the most dominated cell, 20 points behind \gpt at four times the cost.

\paragraph{Tiers separate the strategies, then the models.}
\tone is nearly solved by every Anthropic and OpenAI model under the scaffold.
\ttwo is where the scaffold first matters: it lifts \opus by 43 points and \geminipro by 54.
\tthree is where the frontier models separate: \gpt 93\%, \sonnet 87\%, \opus 82\%.

\paragraph{Wrong drawings, not broken code.}
Most failures are clean, rendered diagrams with the wrong geometry.
The grader catches them.
A person glancing at the picture often would not.

\paragraph{Portability.}
\structured needs schema-constrained decoding.
Our schema compiles to thousands of decoder states, and one vendor's API refuses it before the model writes a token.
That is a gap in the API, not the model, and a warning for any benchmark built on a large schema.

\section{What this means}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Exact grading is practical.}
Every drawing in \cref{tab:headline} was graded by a program, in microseconds, with the same verdict every time.
A model judge would have cost roughly a thousand times more per drawing and could not have told a right angle from one that looks right.

\paragraph{The scaffold matters more than the model.}
The largest effect in the study is not which model you buy but whether you ask it for a typed representation before it draws.
That representation lets the model check its own work before rendering.
The most expensive model gains the most from it and, once it has it, no longer earns its price.

\paragraph{The grader errs in one direction only.}
Every drawing it fails is wrong.
About one pass in six is not as right as it looks, almost always on \ttwo, where the checks confirm the construction but not the numbers the prompt gave.
That lenience applies to every model on the same prompts, so the rankings hold even where the absolute rates are a little high.
Numeric checks for those templates are the planned fix.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\GeoGenBench tests whether a model can turn a sentence into a consistent plane drawing.
It does not test proofs, solids, or teaching.
A third of the templates accept any drawing that meets the stated constraints, not one canonical drawing.
The curriculum prompts are harder for every model than the templated ones, and the headline numbers are templated numbers.

\paragraph{Takeaway.}
A geometry drawing can be graded exactly, from its coordinates, with no judge.
Graded that way, the scaffold matters more than the model.
We release everything needed to run it.
