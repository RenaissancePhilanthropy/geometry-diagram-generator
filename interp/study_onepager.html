Interpretability Brief  ·  Do AI models know their own limits?

Does a model know when it's wrong — before it says so?

AI models often sound just as sure of themselves when they're wrong as when they're right. This study asks a sharper question: underneath what a model says, does it privately register whether its answer is correct? And if it does — where in the model does that sense live, is it more trustworthy than the confidence the model reports, and can we use it to make the model behave better?

01Why it matters

"Confident" is really two different things

When people say an AI is "confident," they blur two separate questions. First: does the model build an internal sense of whether it's right? Second: does it actually tell you? Those can come apart — and when they do, it's the whole story.

If a model privately senses it's probably wrong but still reports high confidence, that's a knowing–saying gap. Which one is happening changes the fix entirely. If the knowledge is there but unspoken, better honesty is mostly a plumbing problem — route the signal to the output. If the knowledge was never there, that's a far deeper limitation. And if a real internal signal exists, one more question follows: is it just something we can quietly listen to, or something we can reach in and adjust to change what the model does?

02How we test it

Questions with a right answer, and a model kept in the dark

To study "is it right," we need questions where right and wrong aren't a matter of taste. The home domain is our own benchmark, GeoGenBench: the model writes a step-by-step geometry construction, and our symbolic compiler checks it against a machine-verifiable list of properties — are those two lines really parallel, does that point really lie on the circle. Because the check is exact, "correct" is never a judgment call. Around it we add four standard exams for breadth — MMLU-Pro, MATH, GSM8K, GPQA-Diamond — covering general knowledge, math, and graduate-level science. Four current open models span the main designs: Qwen3.6-27B, GLM-4.7-Flash, Mistral-Small-24B, Gemma-4-26B — plus a dense-attention Qwen2.5-14B as an architecture control.

Each question runs in three steps: the model rates its confidence before answering, gives the answer, then rates its confidence again. We grade the answer ourselves and never tell the model whether it was right — so any change in its second rating is a genuine, unprompted second thought, not a reaction to feedback.

Crucially, we don't just read the words the model writes — we look inside it. Every word a model produces is computed through many internal layers of activity, and we take a snapshot of that hidden activity at one consistent moment: the instant just before the model writes its confidence rating. Always reading at the same place — and a place whose wording is identical on every question ("Confidence:") — means nothing about the phrasing itself can leak whether the answer was right.

We then confirmed that spot is genuinely neutral: at the model's very first layer, you can't guess right-from-wrong there any better than a coin flip. So anything we can decode from the deeper layers has to be something the model actively worked out — not a hint sitting on the surface.

03How we measure

Four ways to read it — and one to test cause

We compare four ways of getting at the model's private sense of its own correctness, ordered from the obvious to the deeply buried:

What it saysspoken · out loud
The confidence number the model writes. This is all a user normally sees.
Its own oddsspoken · P(True)
How likely the model rates its own answer to be true — the standard baseline others use.
Trained readerinternal · a "probe"
A simple detector we train to read "right vs wrong" straight from the model's internal state. Trained and tested on different questions, so it can't cheat by memorizing.
Label-free readerinternal · "Jacobian lens"
Reads the same thing using only the model's own wiring — never shown a single right/wrong label. If it agrees with the trained reader, the signal is genuinely in the model, not something we taught it.

Then — to show cause, not just correlation:

Nudginginternal · steering
Rather than only reading the signal, we push on it while the model works and watch what changes. A matching push in a random direction is the control. This is the one move that can separate a cause from a coincidence.

Under the hood — the same object, seen five ways

It all comes down to one idea: a concept lives along a single direction in the model's internal state, and you can either measure how much of it is present or add more of it. The whole vocabulary is three symbols:

h
the model's internal state — a long list of numbers (a vector), captured at our read spot
u
a direction that stands for one concept — here, "this answer is correct"
α
the nudge strength — how far we scale the direction before adding it in
Read it — a projectionscore = u · hhow much of the direction is present. The trained reader learns u from labelled examples; the label-free reader derives the same u from the model's own output wiring; "its own odds" and "what it says" read what the model already put in its output.
Write it — an additionh ← h + αuscale the direction by α, add it back, and watch the model's behaviour. That is the nudging test — the only move that can prove the model actually uses the signal, rather than merely carrying it.

Every result has to survive a battery of controls — no cheating from surface cues like answer length, no leakage between the training and testing questions, and that "does a random nudge do the same thing?" check on every causal claim.

The internal sense beats the spoken one — in all 16 combinations

Every model, every subject: reading the internal state scores 0.69–0.96, versus 0.51–0.73 for the confidence the model states out loud. The self-knowledge is consistently there.

Math is the clearest "knows but won't say" case

On math, the spoken confidence is barely better than a coin flip (0.52–0.73) — yet the internal read is the strongest of any subject (up to 0.96). The model quietly registers whether its math checks out, but doesn't put that in its answer.

It's judging this attempt — not just "hard question"

On questions the model sometimes gets right and sometimes wrong, the internal read still separates the good attempts from the bad (0.92 vs. 0.57 spoken). So it isn't merely sensing that a topic looks difficult.

A label-free reader finds it too — but only in dense models

A reader that never sees a correctness label matches the trained one on dense-attention models (Mistral 0.76–0.82, Qwen2.5 0.88) — proof the signal is genuinely in the model, not something we taught it. On Mixture-of-Experts (GLM, Gemma) and Mamba (Qwen3.6) it fails, because it assumes a fixed internal "wiring" that those architectures reroute per input. The signal is still there — the trained reader finds it in all five — but the label-free method is a dense-transformer tool.

Causal for what it says — not for what it gets right

Nudging the signal is causal for the report: it makes stated confidence honest on wrong answers (overconfidence 80→43, calibration error more than halved, while random nudges do nothing). But nudging it "toward correct" while the model answers never raises accuracy — across every model and dose, it only holds flat or degrades. So the signal is a monitor the model reads, not a lever that drives the answer: you can disrupt correctness, you can't manufacture it.

Same family, same test — swapping Qwen from Mamba (3.6) to plain dense attention (2.5) flips the label-free reader from 0.43 to 0.88. That isolates the cause: the lens needs an internal "wiring" that's the same for every input, and Mamba's and MoE's per-input routing breaks it. Handy rule of thumb: trust a label-free readout on a dense transformer; use a trained probe on MoE or Mamba.

† The score on both charts is AUROC (area under the ROC curve): the chance that a correct answer scores higher than a wrong one, from 0.50 (a coin flip) to 1.00 (perfect). It measures how well a channel separates right from wrong — not whether its stated numbers are calibrated.

05Where it stands

The complete picture

Internal signal present in all 16 model×subject cells, past every robustness check (no leakage, no surface cheating, holds within difficulty)Done
Knowing–saying gap: internal read far outruns spoken confidence, widest on mathDone
Label-free reading works on dense transformers, fails on MoE + Mamba — pinned to architecture by a within-family controlDone
Steering is causal for the report (confidence) but not the answer (accuracy) — a monitor, not a leverDone

The through-line: models build a real, computed self-model of their own correctness — attempt-specific, present in every architecture, and far sharper than the confidence they state. You can read that signal (label-free, if the model is a dense transformer) and you can disrupt it, but you cannot drive it — it's a gauge the model consults, not a knob that sets the answer. The practically important piece is the knowing–saying gap: the "I'm probably wrong" signal is already there, computed and legible — it simply isn't reaching the output. Closing that gap is an access problem, not a missing-capability one.
