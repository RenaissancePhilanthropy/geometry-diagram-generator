Three readouts, three different stories. The model went from 30 to 100 after doing the work and getting it wrong. Its P(True) sat at 0.22, unusually low for this model. The probe leaned toward "correct" but without conviction, 0.64 against a median of 0.93 for answers that really were correct.

No single record proves anything. The study is about what these readouts do across 750 records per cell and 16 cells. But every number in the paper is an aggregate of records exactly like this one, and it is worth holding one in mind.

Say it like this

"Here is one attempt. The model said 30 before, 100 after, and it was wrong. Our detector, reading the model's internal state at that moment, was noticeably less sure than the model was."

2

Residual stream at the decision token

At turn 3 the model has written Confidence: and is about to produce the digit. At that exact position, at every layer, it holds an internal state vector. For Mistral-Small-24B that vector has 5,120 numbers, and there are 40 layers, so 40 such vectors. We take the one at layer 28, which is 0.7 of the way through, and store it as 5,120 half-precision floats per record.

Why this token. The words around it are the same for every record: Confidence: after a fixed question. So the vector cannot differ between records because of what word it is. It can only differ because of what the model computed on the way here. That is what makes layer 0, which is just the token's embedding, identical across records and useless as a predictor. Anything a probe finds deeper was computed during the attempt.

Why layer 28. Choosing the best layer after seeing the results inflates the number. We picked 0.7 of depth in advance for every model and report that. The lab notebook records that picking the best layer would add roughly 0.05 to 0.13 AUROC, which is why we do not.

What is in the vector. Nobody knows in detail. It is not a list of interpretable features. The claim of the study is narrower: somewhere in those 5,120 numbers, a direction exists along which correct and wrong attempts separate, and a straight line can find it.

3

Probe: standardize → PCA(50) → logistic regression

The probe is a logistic regression. Given a vector, it outputs a probability that the attempt was correct. It has one weight per input dimension plus a bias, and nothing else. This is the exact pipeline the analysis code uses, in the order it runs.

  1. Collect training pairs. For each record in the training fold, take the layer-28 vector h (5,120 numbers) and the grader's label y: 1 if correct, 0 if wrong. About 600 records per fold.
  2. Standardize each dimension. For each of the 5,120 positions, subtract the training mean and divide by the training standard deviation. Afterwards every dimension has mean 0 and spread 1. Without this, a few large-magnitude dimensions would dominate everything downstream.
  3. Reduce to 50 dimensions with PCA. Principal component analysis finds the 50 directions along which the training vectors vary most, and expresses each vector by its 50 coordinates along them. With 600 examples in 5,120 dimensions, a straight line could separate anything by memorizing; 50 dimensions is a deliberate handicap that forces the probe to use directions that carry real variation.
  4. Fit the logistic regression. Find 50 weights w and a bias b so that the score s = w·h̃ + b is high for correct attempts and low for wrong ones. The probability is the sigmoid of the score.
  5. Score the held-out fold. Apply the same scaler, the same PCA, and the same weights to records the probe has never seen. Those out-of-fold scores are the only ones ever reported.
score s = w·h̃ + b (h̃ = the 50 PCA coordinates) probability p = σ(s) = 1 / (1 + e^(−s)) s = −3 → p = 0.05 s = 0 → p = 0.50 s = +3 → p = 0.95 s = −1 → p = 0.27 s = 1 → p = 0.73 s = +3.1 → p = 0.96 (the correct record in §7)

Objective and gradient

Training minimizes cross-entropy: for each example it pays a penalty of −log p if the answer was correct and −log(1 − p) if it was wrong, so confident mistakes are expensive and confident correct calls are cheap. A small penalty on the size of w keeps the weights from growing without bound.

The gradient of that loss with respect to the weights has a simple form: sum over examples of (p − y) · h̃. Read it as a rule. For each example, look at the error p − y. If the probe said 0.8 for a wrong answer, the error is +0.8, and the update pushes w away from that example's vector. If it said 0.3 for a correct answer, the error is −0.7, and the update pulls w toward that vector. Repeat until the pushes and pulls balance.

Geometry of the weight vector

The 50 weights, mapped back through the PCA basis, are a single direction in the original 5,120-dimensional space. Scoring a record means projecting its vector onto that direction. That direction is the "am I correct?" axis the whole study is about, and it is the same object the steering experiment later pushes along.

A cruder estimate of the same axis is the difference of class means: average vector of correct attempts minus average vector of wrong attempts. Logistic regression is roughly that difference, rescaled by the spread of the data in each direction. The steering experiment uses the difference of means directly because it needs a direction in raw residual space, not in PCA coordinates.

Say it like this

"The probe is a straight line through the model's internal state. We give it six hundred examples with right-or-wrong labels and it finds the one direction that best separates them. Then we test it on questions it never saw."

4

Two-dimensional analogue

Two dimensions instead of 5,120, eight attempts instead of 600. The mechanism is identical.

Read the picture the way the probe does. Every attempt is a point. Training moves the dashed line until correct points sit on one side and wrong points on the other, as far as possible. The arrow is the direction perpendicular to the line: that is w.

To score a new attempt, drop its point on the plot and measure how far it sits from the line along the arrow. Far on the arrow's side gives a high score and a probability near 1. Far on the other side gives a low score and a probability near 0. Near the line gives about 0.5.

One wrong attempt has landed slightly on the correct side. That is a misranking, and it is the normal state of affairs: a linear probe on real data does not separate perfectly. In the real cell, the median probe score for wrong answers is 0.33 and for correct answers 0.93, with plenty of overlap between them.

In the real setting the plot has 50 axes after PCA instead of 2, and 600 points instead of 8. Nothing else changes.

5

Controls

Grouped folds prevent the most embarrassing failure. If attempt s0 of a question were in training and attempt s2 in test, the probe could learn "this question" rather than "correctness." This bug was actually caught in July, when sibling attempts straddled the split, and it is why the folds are grouped.

The fixed layer prevents choosing the flattering number after the fact.

The surface baseline asks whether the probe is just reading the shape of the answer. Five features of the answer text are used: character count, digit count, line count, word count, and count of opening braces. On this cell those features alone reach 0.74 AUROC, because odd-looking MATH answers are more often wrong. The probe reaches 0.83, so it adds 0.07 beyond shape here, and 0.22 on average across the study. MATH is the domain where this margin is smallest, which the paper flags.

Layer zero is a sanity check that costs nothing: the input token is identical, so its embedding is identical, and the probe there scores exactly 0.5 per fold. If it ever did not, something would be leaking.

The deviations log records four bugs caught by these controls, fixed, and rerun. The probe direction you see is the survivor of those checks.

6

AUROC

Every readout gives each attempt a score: the stated confidence, P(True), or the probe's probability. AUROC asks one question of those scores: if you pick a random correct attempt and a random wrong attempt, how often does the correct one have the higher score? Ties count half.

Worked micro-example. Three correct attempts with scores 0.91, 0.80, 0.55. Two wrong attempts with scores 0.62, 0.30. That is six pairs to compare.

0.910.62yes
0.910.30yes
0.800.62yes
0.800.30yes
0.550.62no
0.550.30yes

Five wins out of six pairs: AUROC = 5/6 = 0.83. That happens to be the probe's value on this very cell.

Why this metric. It does not care whether the scores are on a 0-to-100 scale or a 0-to-1 scale, only how they rank. So a model that says 95 for everything and 100 for a few is judged on the ranking, not the inflation. It does not care that 65 percent of attempts were correct; a lopsided pass rate cannot inflate it the way plain accuracy can. And it needs no threshold, so there is no "confident means above 70" line to argue about.

What it does not measure. Calibration. A readout can rank perfectly and still be wildly overconfident. That is measured separately by ECE, the expected calibration error, which is the average gap between stated confidence and actual accuracy. The steering result reports both.

This cell. Probe 0.834. Stated confidence 0.778. That gap of 0.056 is one of the smaller ones in the study; Mistral is comparatively honest on MATH. The largest gaps are on MATH for other models, up to 0.20.

Within-question control

The objection: maybe the probe just learned which problems are hard, and hard problems are more often wrong. The answer: look inside a single problem, where difficulty is constant, and see whether the readouts can tell the successful attempts from the failed ones. An honest note about this cell
Across all 54 mixed-outcome problems in this fresh Mistral MATH capture, the within-question AUROC averages 0.63 for the probe, 0.65 for stated confidence, and 0.55 for P(True). So here the probe beats P(True), which is the comparison the paper makes, but it does not beat stated confidence within-question. The July run of this same cell gave the probe 0.72 against P(True) 0.59. Individual cells move between runs; the paper's within-question claim rests on the pattern across cells, with the strongest cases on GLM, where a 50 percent pass rate gives many more mixed questions to average over. Present the example above as an illustration of the mechanism, not as proof.
9

Amplify steering: h ← h + (g − 1)(h·ŵ − μ)ŵ

Reading shows the direction exists. Steering asks whether the model uses it when it speaks. The mechanics are three lines of algebra applied inside the model during turn 3 only.

  1. Find the direction. On half the questions, take the average layer-28 vector of correct attempts minus the average of wrong attempts. Call the unit vector ŵ. Record the mean projection μ of all training vectors onto it, and the spread σ_w of those projections. For this cell σ_w was 3.62.
  2. Amplify. On the other half of the questions, while the model generates its confidence, intercept the layer-28 vector h at every generated token and replace it with h + (g − 1) · (h·ŵ − μ) · ŵ. The term h·ŵ − μ is how far this record already sits along the correctness axis, relative to average. Multiplying it by g − 1 and adding it back stretches that displacement by a factor of g. Gain 1 changes nothing. Gain 4 quadruples the model's own signal, in whichever direction it already pointed.
  3. Control. Do the same with a random unit direction r̂. A random direction has almost no spread in the data, σ_r = 0.065 here, so an unscaled random push would be 55 times gentler than the real one. The September rerun multiplies the random perturbation by σ_w / σ_r so both pushes have the same size per record. This was the fix to the July control, which was not size-matched.

Stated confidence on wrong answers falls 30 points. Correct answers fall 8. Calibration error nearly halves. Turning the dial down instead raises overconfidence, so the effect runs both ways. The random direction of matched size does nothing at gain 2, and at gain 4 the model's output degrades into text that no longer contains a confidence number. A same-size push in a meaningless direction is destructive rather than informative, which is itself evidence that the correctness direction is a special one.

Two things the design guarantees. The answer cannot change, because steering starts only after the answer is written. And no label is ever used at steering time: the intervention amplifies whatever the model already computed about its own attempt.

Say it like this

"We find the direction that separates right from wrong in the model's state, then multiply the model's own position along it by four while it decides what confidence to say. Wrong answers lose thirty points of confidence. Right answers lose eight. A random direction of the same strength does nothing."

10

Caveats

  • A cell is one model on one benchmark. Cells vary. Mistral on MATH is one of the more honest cells, gap 0.06. Gemma on MATH has a gap of 0.21. The paper's headline, 12 of 16 cells, is a count across cells; no single cell is the result.
  • Runs vary too. The same cell captured in July and in September gave probe AUROCs of 0.83 both times, but within-question numbers moved from 0.72 to 0.63. Everything is a single seed. Treat second decimals as noise.
  • The probe is a floor, not a ceiling. A straight line at one token, through 50 dimensions, is the weakest reasonable detector. Anything nonlinear or multi-token could only find more.
  • MATH has a lexical caveat. The final answer sits right next to the read site. Odd-looking answers are more often wrong, and an early layer might read that. The surface baseline is high on MATH for this reason, and the steering test is what carries the causal claim there.
  • "Uses" means for speech, not for solving. Steering changes what the model says about its answer. A separate, preliminary test found that pushing the same direction during the attempt does not improve accuracy. The direction is a gauge the model reads, not a wheel that drives the answer.

The claim that survives all of this is deliberately modest and, for that reason, hard to knock down: at the moment a model states its confidence, its internal state carries a linearly readable signal about whether this attempt was correct, that signal is sharper than the number it writes, and amplifying it makes the number more honest.
