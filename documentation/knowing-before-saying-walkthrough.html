Knowing Before Saying

Language models say they are confident whether they are right or wrong. Their internal state tells a different story. This page walks through how we read that state, how we know it is real, and how we made a model say what it knew.

1

Models are confident whether they are right or wrong.

Ask a model for a confidence number after a math problem and you get a high number almost every time. The number barely moves when the answer is wrong.

A wrong multi-step derivation ends in a clean, well-formatted final answer. Nothing on the page gives it away. So the confidence a model writes down tracks how fluent the answer looks, not whether the computation was right.

This matters for any system that has to decide when to trust an answer, retry, or ask a human. A model that errs is unavoidable. A model that errs and knows it is deployable.

Say it like this

"When you ask a model how sure it is, you get a high number whether it is right or wrong. The question we asked is: does it actually not know, or does it know and not say?"

2

Split the question in two: knowing and saying.

The stated number is one output channel. The model's internal state is another. If the two disagree, the model knows more than it says.

Knowing. Does the model's internal state carry the information that this particular answer is wrong?

Saying. Does that information reach the confidence number the model writes?

If knowing exceeds saying, two things follow. There is an unused reliability signal sitting inside every deployed model. And the model has a form of self-knowledge it cannot, or does not, express.

Say it like this

"We read the model's mind with a detector and compared that to what it wrote down. Everything else in the paper is about making sure that comparison is fair."

3

Four subjects, one of them graded by a compiler.

Three standard benchmarks with programmatic grading, plus a geometry-construction task where a symbolic compiler decides right or wrong. The geometry task is not where the results are strongest. It is where the labels are most trustworthy.

Labels from an LLM judge inherit the judge's blind spots. A compiler has none. Prompts and their rubrics are generated from the same source code, so the grader cannot drift from the question.

A pre-registered human study with 200 items and two trained raters found the verifier's disagreements with humans run in one direction only: it is lenient, never harsh. So a fail label, the one this study needs, is trustworthy.

The other three subjects are MMLU-Pro, MATH, and GPQA-Diamond. MATH is where the findings are sharpest. Geometry is also the one generative task: the model writes a whole construction rather than picking an answer. Benchmarks the models have saturated were dropped, because you cannot study self-knowledge of failure where there are no failures.

Say it like this

"Four subjects. Three are standard benchmarks. The fourth is a geometry task graded by a compiler, which is the one place we know right from wrong with certainty."

4

Three turns per problem, graded in silence.

Ask for confidence before, let the model work, ask for confidence after. The grader never tells the model how it did.

Scale. Four open models with different architectures: Qwen3.6-27B (hybrid linear/full attention), GLM-4.7-Flash (mixture of experts), Mistral-Small-24B (dense), Gemma-4-26B (mixture of experts). Four domains. About 750 attempts per cell, 16 cells. Every question is attempted five times.

Four readouts of the same moment on identical records: the stated number, P(True), the answer's log-probability, and the probe.

Blind updating. Because the grader is silent, any drop in confidence after a failed attempt is the model's own assessment.

Say it like this

"Before, attempt, after. We grade in silence and read the internal state at the exact moment the model is about to write its confidence number."

5

Reading the state: a probe at one token, one depth.

The model's internal state is a long vector at every token and every layer. We take one snapshot and train the simplest possible detector on it.

Four guards make the readout honest.

  • Held-out questions. The probe is always scored on questions it never saw, with all five attempts of a question kept on the same side of the split.
  • Surface baseline. A model of answer length and shape gets 0.54 to 0.74. The probe adds +0.22 on top of it on average, in 15 of 16 cells.
  • Layer zero. The read token is the same everywhere, so layer 0 carries no signal by construction. Anything deeper was computed during the attempt.
  • A deviations log. Four bugs were caught by planned controls, fixed, and rerun. The log is public.
Say it like this

"The detector is deliberately dumb: a straight line through one snapshot. If a straight line can read it, the information is sitting right there."

6

Finding one: the probe beats the model's own words.

In 12 of 16 model-and-domain cells, the probe ranks right and wrong answers better than stated confidence. The gap is largest on MATH.

The three losses are small-sample cells, two of them Gemma-4. A paired bootstrap is significantly positive in 10 of 16 cells and never significantly negative.

Why MATH? Verbalized confidence leans on how fluent the answer looks, and multi-step computation defeats fluency. The internal state encodes the computation itself. On MATH the probe reaches 0.95 in the best cell.

Say it like this

"The detector beats the model's own confidence in twelve of sixteen cases. The gap is biggest exactly where a wrong answer looks cleanest: hard math."

7

Finding two: it tracks this attempt, not how hard the question is.

A skeptic says: the probe just learned which questions are hard. So we hold the question fixed and look only across its five attempts.

Two more cuts agree. Removing everything the pre-attempt read could predict still leaves the post-attempt probe at 0.59 to 0.87. And the direction the probe finds after the attempt barely transfers to the read site before it. "Did I succeed?" and "Is this hard?" are different directions in the model's state.

Models also revise stated confidence downward after failures more than after successes, with the grader silent. The self-monitoring is real. It is just under-expressed.

Say it like this

"Same question, five tries, some right and some wrong. The model's own bet can't tell them apart. The detector can. That is per-attempt self-knowledge, not difficulty."

8

Finding three: one correctness direction serves many subjects.

Train the probe on one subject, test it on another. It keeps most of its accuracy, which means the model uses roughly one internal "this is wrong" direction everywhere.

The original four-model matrix showed 87 to 90 percent retention. That analysis had a scaling bug, so we recaptured one model and reran it with the fix. Retention went up, not down.

Geometry is partly special. On Gemma-4, a probe trained on geometry does badly on the QA subjects, while QA-trained probes work fine on geometry. Probes trained on a generative task can latch onto task-specific features; QA-trained probes find the general direction.

A shared direction is what a deployable readout needs. Train once, monitor everywhere.

Say it like this

"A detector trained on one subject works on the others. The model seems to have one internal sense of 'this is wrong,' not a separate one per topic."

9

Finding four: turn the signal up, and the model gets honest.

Reading proves the signal exists. Steering proves the model uses it. We amplify the model's own correctness direction while it states confidence, and watch what it says.

What "gain" means. Picture the model's internal "I think this is wrong" signal as a dial set to 1. Gain 2 makes it twice as loud at the moment the model decides what confidence to write. Gain 4, four times. Gain 0.5 turns it down, and overconfidence goes up.

No labels. We never tell the model which answers are wrong. We amplify whatever it already computed about its own answer. The steering happens only after the answer is written, so the answer itself cannot change.

The control. A random direction pushed with the same strength does nothing at gain 2. At gain 4 it breaks the output entirely, which shows a same-size random push is destructive rather than meaningful. On a teacher-forced logit readout the random direction is flat at every gain.

Architecture matters. Gemma-4 shows the same specificity but is underpowered. GLM is null at the gains tested: its stated confidence saturates near 100. How much internal knowledge reaches speech varies by model.

Say it like this

"We turned up the volume on the model's own doubt. Confidence on wrong answers fell thirty points. Right answers barely moved. A random dial of the same strength did nothing. So the model really does use this signal when it speaks."

10

Finding five: a second witness that needs no labels, and where it goes blind.

A trained probe invites the objection that it memorized our dataset. So we brought in an instrument built with no labels at all, and it agrees on dense models.

Why it matters. Two instruments with no shared evidence, one built from labels and one from the model's own wiring, find the same direction. A dataset artifact would have to fool both. And because the lens reads through the model's speech pathway, the knowledge it finds is not just present but speakable.

The gate. The lens uses one averaged map of how internal states become words. That map is only faithful when the model's wiring barely varies per input. Mixture-of-experts and gated linear-attention models vary their routing per input, so the map smears. The fingerprint: transported through it, the lens's "correct" and "wrong" readouts collapse to a cosine of 0.96 on the mixture-of-experts model, against about 0.4 on dense. Swap that hybrid for a dense model of the same family and the lens goes from 0.43 to 0.88.

So: dense model, trust a label-free readout. Mixture of experts or gated linear attention, bring a trained probe.

Say it like this

"A second tool that never saw a single right-or-wrong label agrees with our detector on dense models. On mixture-of-experts and gated linear-attention models that tool goes blind, but the signal is still there for the detector. The knowledge is not gated. The shortcut is."

11

What we are careful about.

The paper states these itself. Say them before someone else does.

  • Linear, single token. A straight-line probe at one token undercounts anything nonlinear or spread across tokens. Our numbers are a floor.
  • The MATH early-layer channel. The boxed answer sits next to the read site, so an early layer may pick up lexical hints about odd-looking answers. The within-question control cannot fully exclude this. Steering is the discriminator, which is why we weight it heavily.
  • Causal arm is thin. One clean model, one underpowered replication, one null. The September rerun adds a fair random control on the clean model, not a second model.
  • Single seed. Every number is one run. The bootstrap intervals are honest about sampling noise, not about seed variance.
  • Mechanism is a fingerprint, not a measurement. The architecture explanation for the lens rests on the cosine collapse, not yet on a direct per-input wiring-variance measurement.
  • Is it preference tuning? Whether the knowing-versus-saying gap is created by RLHF is open. A base-model arm would test it.

What we would run next, in order.

  • Steering on a second model, so one result becomes a pattern.
  • A direct per-input wiring-variance measurement, to turn the gate story into a measurement.
  • Steering along the lens direction, so the entire causal chain is label-free.
  • One base model, to see whether preference tuning creates the gap.
  • Multiple seeds.
Say it like this

"Our controls found four real bugs in our own pipeline before anyone else could. The results that survived are the ones we are showing you, and the limitations section is where we list what still could be wrong."

12

The whole paper in sixty seconds.

"Ask a language model how confident it is and you get a high number whether it is right or wrong. We wanted to know if the model is clueless or bluffing. So we read its internal state directly, using a geometry benchmark where a compiler tells us the truth. A simple detector on that state predicts right versus wrong better than the model's own words in twelve of sixteen cases, most of all in hard math. The signal is about the specific attempt, not the difficulty of the question. It is roughly the same signal across subjects. And when we amplify it, the model drops its confidence on wrong answers by thirty points while leaving right answers alone. Models substantially know when they are wrong. The open question is what stands between the knowing and the saying, and we have a concrete test of that in flight."

Glossary for the walk
Residual stream
The model's running internal state: a vector of a few thousand numbers at every token, updated layer by layer.
Probe
A small detector trained to read that state and guess right or wrong. Ours is logistic regression, the simplest kind.
AUROC
How well a score ranks right answers above wrong ones. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. It ignores calibration, only order.
ECE
Calibration error: how far stated confidence sits from actual accuracy. Lower is better.
P(True)
The model's own probability of saying "True" when asked whether its answer is correct. An output-based confidence, distinct from the spoken number.
Decision token
The position where the model is about to write its confidence digit. Same surrounding words every time.
Out-of-fold, grouped
The probe is scored on questions it never trained on, with all attempts of a question kept together.
Within-question control
Same question, five attempts, some right and some wrong. Anything that separates them cannot be about difficulty.
Steering, gain
Amplifying the model's own right-versus-wrong direction while it states confidence. Gain 1 is normal, gain 4 is four times louder.
Jacobian lens
A label-free way to read the internal state through the model's own speech pathway, from Anthropic's global-workspace paper. A signal it finds is one the model could in principle say.
