\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:motivation}

Educational testing runs on a steady supply of fresh items, and writing each one by hand---together with the key used to score it---is slow, expensive, and a bottleneck every testing program feels. For decades the field's answer has been \emph{automatic item generation} (AIG): an author writes a single template, or \emph{item model}, and a computer mints many parallel items from it, each carrying its own answer key \citep{bejar2002generative,gierl2013aig}. AIG has worked best for items built from text; the ones it has struggled to reach are those whose response is a \emph{picture}. In K--12 mathematics, the picture that matters most is the geometry diagram.

Producing a correct one is harder than it looks. Take a simple request:

\begin{quote}
\emph{``Draw an acute triangle $ABC$ with $\angle A = 60^\circ$ and $\angle B = 70^\circ$, then draw the altitude from $C$, meeting $AB$ at $H$.''}
\end{quote}

To be correct, the drawing has to get several things \emph{exactly} right: angle $A$ is $60^\circ$, the point $H$ lies on the segment $AB$ (not past its end), and the altitude meets $AB$ at a true right angle. If a model produces a drawing by guessing coordinates, these facts hold only by luck. And when they fail, they fail silently: the result still looks like a triangle with a line through it, even if that right angle is really $88^\circ$ or the foot of the altitude slipped just past $B$.

The same exactness makes such diagrams hard to score. Almost every geometry benchmark runs the task backwards---it shows the model a finished diagram and asks a question about it \citep{lu2021intergps,lu2024mathvista}---so none checks whether a model can \emph{produce} a correct figure in the first place. The benchmarks that do score generated pictures were built for everyday images and rely on an AI ``judge'' that looks at the output \citep{huang2025t2icompbenchpp,wang2025genexam}. A judge can say a shape \emph{looks like} a right triangle, but it cannot certify that an angle is exactly $90^\circ$. For geometry diagrams, then, neither making the item nor scoring it comes for free.

Our idea is to stop guessing and stop looking. We have the model describe the construction in words---``$H$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $AB$''---and hand that description to a geometry engine that computes the exact coordinates. The model chooses \emph{what} to build; the engine works out \emph{where} every point goes. That single move answers both problems at once:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Better drawings.} The hard arithmetic is done by a solver, so a right angle comes out exactly right instead of merely close.
\item \textbf{Automatic scoring.} Because the finished figure is now exact numbers, we can check each requirement directly---``is this angle $90^\circ$?''---rather than asking a judge what the picture looks like.
\end{itemize}

That is this paper's contribution: a \emph{machine-verifiable item-model framework} for geometry diagrams. From a single construction, one item model emits both the prompt and a \emph{computed} answer key---a machine-checkable list of geometric requirements---and the figure produced in response is verified against that key. We make two empirical claims and are deliberate about their strength: structured construction improves how often models get the geometry right (\cref{sec:benchmark}), but human validation shows the automatic score is best read as an \emph{upper bound} on correctness, not a validated high-stakes score (\cref{sec:scoring}). A fuller, per-cell leaderboard and cost analysis appear in a companion paper; here the benchmark is evidence for these two claims, not the contribution itself.

The rest of the paper walks through how the model describes a diagram (\cref{sec:pipeline}), how scoring follows from that same description (\cref{sec:scoring}), what we found running it on six large models (\cref{sec:benchmark}), and why this is useful for educational measurement (\cref{sec:measurement}).
