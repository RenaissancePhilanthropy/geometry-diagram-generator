\section{Putting it to the test}
\label{sec:benchmark}

To show the approach works beyond a single example, we evaluate on \GeoGenBench, 801 prompts, all scored automatically by the checker of \cref{sec:scoring}. Six hundred come from the templates described above; another 201 are drawn from a real K--12 mathematics curriculum \citep{bluebonnet2024}. We will release the prompt set, templates, checker vocabulary, and rendered outputs upon publication. We ran six large models from three companies, each in two modes: writing drawing code by hand, and using the structured description. Throughout, a \emph{pass} means passing that automatic checker; since the checker is one-directionally lenient (\cref{sec:scoring}), the rates below are optimistic ceilings on true geometric correctness, not exact-correctness rates.

\paragraph{How we ran it.}
The by-hand baseline asks the model to write TikZ drawing code directly; the structured mode fills in the typed form of \cref{sec:pipeline}. Both modes run at temperature $1.0$, with no explicit output-token limit and the \emph{same} budget of up to three attempts---what differs is the retry trigger. The baseline retries only when its code fails to render; the structured mode also retries when its description fails to compile or the figure breaks a check it declared. Those declared checks are the model's own, though: the held-out scoring predicates the item template generates---the ones we actually grade against---are never fed back to it. The structured advantage is therefore not more attempts but more feedback---it lets a model catch its own geometric mistakes, which hand-written code cannot, and those extra retries show up in its per-item cost. Each requirement is decided on the computed coordinates within an absolute tolerance of $\tau = 5\times10^{-3}$ (coordinates normalised to $[-10, 10]$). The $600$ templated prompts divide evenly across three difficulty tiers ($200$ each), generated from $30$ construction templates---triangles, quadrilaterals, circles, segments, and lines---with $3.2$ requirements apiece on average; the $201$ curriculum prompts were written from the topic scaffold of a public K--12 geometry curriculum (drafted with a language model, answer keys written by hand) and carry more, $6.0$ on average. Cost per item is the prompt's token count, summed across any retries and priced at each vendor's published rates, then averaged over every prompt---failures included. Failures are dominated by wrong geometry---a requirement that simply does not hold---rather than broken drawing code, and the structured mode's remaining failures skew toward timeouts.

The structured approach helped \emph{every} model (\cref{tab:results})---and helped the weakest ones the most, raising pass rates by anywhere from 2 to 25 percentage points. In other words, handing a model the geometry engine matters most when the model is poor at drawing by hand. More striking still: once they used the structured approach, the three strongest models landed within a single point of one another, even though they differ sevenfold in price. On this task, the method does more of the work than the model does.

We also hit one practical snag worth flagging for anyone building something similar: Gemini~2.5~Flash refused our structured form outright for being too complex, so the structured mode could not run there at all. Additional per-template results, cost breakdowns, and failure analyses appear in the companion paper. Here they make a single point: structured construction helps at scale, across every model we tried.
