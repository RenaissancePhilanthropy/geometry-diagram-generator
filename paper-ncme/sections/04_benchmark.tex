\section{Putting it to the test}
\label{sec:benchmark}

To show the approach works beyond a single example, we built \GeoGenBench: 801 prompts, all scored automatically by the checker of \cref{sec:scoring}. Six hundred come from the templates described above; another 201 are drawn from a real K--12 mathematics curriculum \citep{bluebonnet2024}. We ran six large models from three companies, each in two modes: writing drawing code by hand, and using the structured description.

The structured approach helped \emph{every} model (\cref{tab:results})---and helped the weakest ones the most, raising pass rates by anywhere from 2 to 25 percentage points. In other words, handing a model the geometry engine matters most when the model is poor at drawing by hand. More striking still: once they used the structured approach, the three strongest models landed within a single point of one another, even though they differ sevenfold in price. On this task, the method does more of the work than the model does.

We also hit one practical snag worth flagging for anyone building something similar: Gemini~2.5~Flash refused our structured form outright for being too complex, so the structured mode could not run there at all. The full results---a complete leaderboard, cost comparisons, and the human study---appear in a companion paper. Here they make a single point: generation-then-scoring holds up at scale.
