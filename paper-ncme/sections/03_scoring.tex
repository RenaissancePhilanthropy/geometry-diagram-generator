\section{Automatic scoring from shared construction logic}
\label{sec:scoring}

Because the finished diagram is just exact coordinates, many of a prompt's requirements can be scored directly, as explicit geometric predicates. ``Is the angle at $H$ a right angle?'' is settled by a one-line calculation on the coordinates---in a fraction of a millisecond---not by asking a judge whether the picture looks square. This makes scoring inspectable and inexpensive, but not complete: the checklist's validity still depends on whether it captures everything the prompt asks for and the visual conventions a reader expects---limits we examine below.

\paragraph{The question and its answer key are written together.}
Here is the move that makes this practical: each item and its answer key come from the \emph{same} short program (\cref{fig:cogen}). One template produces both the wording shown to the model and the checklist used to score it. Because the two are emitted together, they are far less likely to drift apart through manual editing---though template-level errors remain possible: an author can still omit a predicate, encode the wrong one, or write wording whose intended constraint the checklist does not fully capture. Re-running the template with a new random seed still mints a fresh, parallel item, and the checklist is never shown to the model while it works.

\paragraph{A small, readable checklist.}
Every requirement is written in a small, fixed vocabulary of checks (\cref{tab:predicates}): is this a right angle, is this the midpoint, are these lines parallel, does this point lie on that segment, and so on. There are 15 such checks in the current version, and each is decided exactly on the coordinates (within a tiny rounding tolerance). A diagram passes only if every check on its list passes. The list is, in plain terms, the definition of what the item is testing.

\paragraph{Does the automatic score match a human's?}
An automatic score is only worth having if it agrees with a careful human grader, so we checked. After fixing a few blind spots found on pilot data, we ran a pre-registered study. We set the bar in advance: the automatic score had to match the human verdict at Cohen's $\kappa \ge 0.70$ (on a scale where $1.0$ is perfect agreement and $0$ is chance). Two graduate-level graders then scored 200 diagrams by hand, without seeing the automatic verdict.

We did not clear that bar. Agreement was only moderate---Cohen's $\kappa = 0.46$, well short of the pre-registered $0.70$---and we treat this as a central limitation, not a footnote. The disagreements do run \emph{one way}: in 36 of the 200 cases the automatic scorer passed a diagram the graders failed, and in \emph{zero} cases was it too harsh. That one-sidedness is good for fairness---no model is penalised unjustly---but it is the \emph{dangerous} direction once a score is read as a certificate of correctness, because every disagreement is a false positive. We therefore state it plainly: \textbf{the model pass rates we report in \cref{sec:benchmark} are optimistic ceilings}---upper bounds on how often the geometry is truly right, not the rate itself. The errors do concentrate in one place---items with an added construction (like our altitude), where the checklist confirms the construction but does not separately re-check the prompt's exact angles---and the two graders agreed with \emph{each other} at the $0.70$ we had asked the scorer to reach ($\kappa = 0.70$). So the gap is in the checklist, not the human ratings; the fix, adding the missing numeric checks, is straightforward and underway, and until it lands the reported rates should be read as ceilings.

\paragraph{What the checks cannot see.}
That particular gap closes with more checks. A deeper one does not. The checks read the geometry---the coordinates---and nothing else; they never look at how the figure is drawn. A diagram can satisfy every requirement and still mislead a reader: a right-angle mark placed on the outside of the corner, an angle labelled on the wrong side, or a figure that passes every check yet is laid out so awkwardly that no reader could follow it. This is the mirror image of the AI judge from \cref{sec:motivation}. A judge sees how a picture looks but cannot check its geometry; our checks test that geometry but cannot see how it looks. The two are complementary: the checks speak to whether a diagram is \emph{correct}, while whether it is \emph{clear} is still best judged by eye.
