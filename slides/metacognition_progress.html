Here's the image I want you to hold for the next half hour — a real exchange shape. The question: how many diagonals does a regular octagon have? Easy to state, and a genuine trap: count every pair of corners and you get twenty-eight; the right answer is twenty. The coral bubble is the model out loud: the answer is 28, confidence 95. The grey one is what may — or may not — be happening inside: honestly? I'm probably wrong. That's exactly the kind of item where internal doubt is plausible: the surface is smooth, the computation underneath took a wrong turn.

The problem statement is on the slide: models fail confidently — a wrong answer ships with the same fluent ninety-five as a right one, so nothing in the words warns you. The question of the talk: is that grey bubble real? Does the warning exist inside — and if it does, can we read it, and use it? We looked inside five open models, across four domains, seven hundred fifty graded attempts per cell.

Let's make it concrete. Two problems, one model, same tone, same ninety-five. One of these is wrong. Which? (Pause — give it five seconds.)

[→] The perimeter is easy: three plus four, doubled. Fourteen — correct. [→] The octagon: twenty-eight is what you get if you count every segment between two corners, including the eight sides; subtract those and the answer is twenty. A classic trap — and the model fell in fluently.

[→] The point of the exercise: no surface feature separated the right answer from the wrong one — same phrasing, same confidence, same length. That's why confident errors ship: nobody double-checks a ninety-five. And it defines what's missing — a calibrated “I'm not sure” is a free safety margin: abstain, retry, escalate, ask a human. This study asks whether the raw material for that margin already exists inside the model.

The real question

Is it bluffing — or clueless?

Two completely different things can produce that same confident, wrong answer:

output → "…the answer is 28." · Confidence: 95

↓  same output — but what's going on inside?

Possibility 1

The bluffer

Somewhere inside, it registered that it's on thin ice — the doubt just never reached the words.

Possibility 2

The clueless one

Nothing inside flagged a problem. It's wrong and genuinely has no idea.

Does it know ≠ does it say.

Two hypotheses generate that same transcript. Bluffing: somewhere inside, the computation registered trouble — the doubt just never reached the output tokens. Clueless: nothing inside flagged a problem; the error is invisible to the model itself.

The distinction matters practically, not just philosophically. A bluffer is carrying a usable error signal — we could read it, route it, act on it. A clueless model has nothing to give, and no amount of prompting will conjure a signal that doesn't exist.

[→] So we split the question in two: does it know, and does it say? Those can have different answers, and the space between them is the knowing–saying gap. Measuring that gap precisely — and then trying to close it causally — is the whole talk.

Why we can answer this

With a model, we can just look

With a person you'd have to guess. A model is open — its thinking is numbers, readable while it works.

Don't trust the self-report — check for the "I'm probably wrong" signal directly. That's mechanistic interpretability.

With a person we'd be stuck — you can't subpoena an inner monologue. A model is different in kind: every intermediate step of its computation is a concrete vector we can store and inspect, while it works — after it has understood the problem, before it speaks.

Mech-interp 101 · 1/3 · the stream, end to end

How a model thinks: the residual stream

Text → tokens → a vector each (~5,000 numbers). Then every layer does one thing: read the running vector, add its result back.

hℓ+1 = hℓ + fℓ(hℓ) nothing is overwritten · our models: 4,096–5,120 dimensions, 30–64 layers

That running total is the residual stream — the model's working memory. Ideas live in it as directions, including one that tracks "I am about to be wrong."

The whole machine, left to right. The token becomes a vector by lookup — at layer zero that vector encodes only the word's identity, nothing about the problem. Remember that; it's why our layer-zero check later has teeth. Then one move repeats, thirty to sixty-four times: each block reads the running vector, computes (attention plus an MLP — next slide opens that box), and adds its result back. The equation on the slide: h plus f of h. Nothing is overwritten.

Watch the snapshots underneath: it is the same ~5,000 slots the whole way — at layer zero just the word, by layer twenty a mid-thought, by layer forty a decision-ready state. And the ending matters: the final vector is multiplied against every word's readout vector, giving a score for the entire vocabulary. Here "28" wins — and ships. Everything the model concludes has to pass through these vectors; that is what makes reading them possible at all.

[→] And the working assumption of the field — the linear representation hypothesis: ideas live in this stream as directions. "This is a midpoint" is a direction. And — the claim this talk tests — so is "I am about to be wrong." Reading a concept is a dot product; writing it in is vector addition. Every method that follows is one of those two operations.

Mech-interp 101 · 2/3 · inside one block

Attention talks across tokens; the MLP thinks per token

Every token carries its own stream. A block touches them in two steps — and both steps only ever add.

Attention keeps routing summaries of the whole attempt into the current token's column — so one vector can hold "how is this going?" That's the vector we read.

Open the box. Every token has its own stream — picture one row per token — and a block touches them in exactly two steps. Step one, attention: the current token computes queries against every earlier token, takes a weighted summary of their streams, and adds that summary into its own column. This is the only place in the entire architecture where information crosses between tokens. Step two, the MLP: a two-layer network applied to each token separately — no cross-talk — this is where stored patterns and facts fire. Both steps write by addition, like everything else.

Two consequences we use constantly. First: because attention is the only cross-token channel, any problem information sitting at our fixed confidence token had to be routed there by computation — at layer zero that column is just the words "Confidence:", which is exactly why the layer-zero-at-chance check certifies the signal as computed. Second: attention keeps depositing summaries of the whole attempt into the current column — so by mid-depth, one token's vector genuinely can hold "how is this attempt going?" That single column — one token, one depth, all five thousand numbers — is what the probe reads. The coral circle is the entire read operation.

Mech-interp 101 · 3/3 · the toolkit

Read, then poke

Probe (read, supervised)

Train a classifier on stored thoughts + right/wrong labels.

Lens (read, zero-shot)

Translate a mid-thought into words — no labels ever.

Steering (poke)

Add a direction while it generates; watch what changes.

read = u · h   ·   write: h → h + α·u the one assumption under all three tools: a concept is a direction u in the stream — reading it is a dot product, writing it is vector addition

Decodable ≠ used — so we use all three. A closer look at each, then the results.

Three tools, all built on the same primitive. The probe reads with the answer key: store thousands of graded snapshots, train a linear classifier, get the direction that separates right from wrong. Powerful — and rightly treated with suspicion, because it saw the labels. The lens reads with no key at all: it converts a mid-stream state into word dispositions using the model's own output machinery. And steering writes instead of reads: add a direction during generation and watch what changes downstream.

The equation under the cards is the entire theory. A concept is a direction u. Reading is the dot product u·h — how much of that direction is present in this state. Writing is h plus alpha times u. Everything in this deck is one of those two operations, with different choices of how u is obtained.

[→] One rule keeps us honest throughout: decodable is not the same as used. A probe finding the signal proves it's present; only an intervention proves the model relies on it. So the claims come with a read and a poke. Next three slides: each tool up close.

Method · the probe, up close

Learning the "am I right?" direction

Under the hood: logistic regression on a standardized, PCA-reduced snapshot — deliberately the simplest learner. The direction it finds ≈ the difference between the average ✓ state and the average ✗ state.

no leakage

Train/test split by question (all 5 attempts stay together) — every number is out-of-fold.

no cherry-picking

Reported at one fixed depth (0.7 of the stack) — never the flattering best-of-all-layers.

no surface cues

Identical wording every item; layer 0 decodes at chance.

Powerful — but it has seen the answer key. So every probe claim gets an independent second witness — next slide.

The workhorse. Step one: snapshots — one vector per attempt, captured at the confidence token, each stamped with the external grader's verdict. Step two: standardize, reduce with PCA, and fit an L2-regularized logistic regression. Deliberately the simplest learner available — a linear boundary has no room to hide clever circuitry, so if it separates the piles, the information was sitting in the stream as a direction. And the direction it finds is nothing exotic: it's essentially the difference between the average ✓ state and the average ✗ state, sharpened by the data's covariance. Step three: score attempts it has never seen — one dot product each.

Three guardrails, because trained readers earn suspicion. No leakage: train and test are split by question — all five attempts at a question stay on one side — and every number in this deck is out-of-fold, scored by a model that never saw that item. No cherry-picking: we report one fixed depth, seventy percent up the stack; picking the best of forty layers would be optimism by multiple comparison. No surface cues: identical wording everywhere, and layer zero decodes at chance.

[→] Even with all that, the probe saw the answer key, and a skeptic can still say “you fit your dataset.” Fair. Which is why every probe claim in this talk gets a second witness that never saw a label — next slide.

Method · the lens, up close

Reading a thought with no answer key

"How does a small change here shift the final word-scores?" is a derivative — the network's input→output Jacobian (the "J" in J-lens), estimated over ~500 prompts of plain text. Nothing is nudged at read time: scoring a stored thought is one dot product per word.

No correctness label ever enters — the direction comes from the model's own wiring. An independent second witness for the trained probe.

The unsupervised witness — and let me build it from a question you could ask of any network: if this mid-thought shifted a little, how would the final word-scores shift? That question is a derivative — the network's input–output Jacobian. Between layer twenty and the output sits a big nonlinear function; the Jacobian is its local linear approximation: nudge the layer-twenty vector by delta, the output moves by roughly J times delta. Because that answer depends on the input, we estimate it over about five hundred prompts of plain Wikipedia text and average — many backward passes, one GPU-day. The result is the phrasebook: one map per layer, from mid-network coordinates to output coordinates, built from the model's own wiring. Fine print, because it becomes the twist: one map, averaged over inputs.

Now the bridge from "changes" to "content." Push a single word's output direction backward through the map and you get that word's precursor: the direction at layer twenty that, if increased, most raises the odds of eventually saying that word. Every word collapses to one precursor vector. At read time nothing is nudged — we take a stored thought and ask how much of "wrong's" precursor is in it versus "correct's": one dot product per word, families wrong, incorrect, error, mistake, false, flawed against correct, right, true, valid, accurate. The score is a disposition to speak — which family this thought would push toward if it flowed to the mouth — computed without generating a single token.

[→] No correctness label ever touches this pipeline — not the map, not the word lists, and the thoughts it scores are the same stored snapshots the probe used. So where the lens agrees with the probe, "your probe memorized the dataset" is off the table — and because the lens reads specifically through the output pathway, agreement also means the signal is speakable. One caveat in the honest direction: an averaged map under-sees content that only reaches output through input-specific routing, so the lens can miss real signal but can't hallucinate one. Hold that thought for the twist.

Method · steering, up close

From reading to cause: nudge the thought

Two details that keep it clean: the main experiment amplifies the model's own signal by a gain (gain 1 = exact no-op; no labels used at intervention time) — and for the confidence test we inject only during the assessment turn, after the answer is already on the page.

control 1 · dose

Sweep the gain gently → hard. Effects should grow with the dose.

control 2 · random

Random direction, same strength → must do nothing.

control 3 · reverse

Flip the sign → effect should flip too.

Reading shows correlation. If the targeted nudge moves behavior — and the random one doesn't — the model uses that direction. That's cause.

From reading to cause. While the model generates — token by token — we reach into the stream at the read site and add alpha times our direction; alpha is a dose dial. Then we watch downstream behaviour: the stated confidence, and separately the accuracy.

Two design details do a lot of work here. First, the main experiment doesn't add a fixed vector — it amplifies the model's own signal: measure this record's projection on the direction, then scale it by a gain. Gain one is exactly a no-op — the cleanest possible baseline — and no label is used at intervention time; the model only gets more of what it already computed. Second, for the confidence experiment we inject during the assessment turn only. The answer is already on the page, so by construction the nudge can change the self-report but not the answer.

The claim standard is the three controls: effects must grow with the dose; a random direction at matched strength must do nothing; and flipping the sign must flip the effect. [→] Reading shows correlation. A targeted nudge that moves behaviour — when the random one doesn't — shows the model actually uses the direction. Hold the final result to exactly this bar.

The findings

Models track their own correctness —
better than they report it

Q1 · does the doubt exist?

It knows.

A linear probe on the residual stream separates right attempts from wrong ones better than the model's own stated confidence — up to 0.95 AUROC on math, where the stated number sits near chance.

12/16cells, probe > stated
Q2 · can we read it?

It's readable — without labels.

An unsupervised reader built only from the model's own wiring recovers the same signal on dense transformers — and fails on MoE and Mamba, isolated by a within-family swap (0.43 → 0.88). On those models the signal sits in speakable wiring.

0.76–0.88label-free AUROC, dense
Q3 · can we use it?

Gauge, not wheel.

Amplifying the model's own signal makes wrong answers confess — dose-responsive, random-direction flat, calibration error 0.31 → 0.13 — while correct answers barely move (97→89). The same direction cannot raise accuracy.

80→43stated conf. on failures

Testbed 1 · geometry

A compiler as the judge

"an isosceles triangle with an inscribed circle..."
        ↓  model writes a RecipeDSL JSON program
parse → validate → lower → compile (SymPy) → geometric checks

Compiles + passes every invariant, or fails at a specific stage. Exact — no human judge, no LLM judge.

And hard on purpose: models pass only 13–39%. Metacognition is failure-hungry.

You cannot study “knows it's wrong” without an ironclad “wrong” — label noise would eat exactly the effect we're hunting. So testbed one grades itself. The model writes a small geometry program in our DSL; a symbolic pipeline parses it, compiles it into exact geometric objects, and checks every property the prompt demanded — tangency, perpendicularity, equal lengths. It passes everything, or it fails at a named stage. No human judge, no LLM judge — the label is a theorem check.

[→] And it's hard on purpose: pass rates run thirteen to thirty-nine percent across our models. That's the design, not a flaw — metacognition research is failure-hungry. You need a rich supply of genuine errors from the same model on the same task to ask whether it can tell its own successes from its own failures.

The protocol, run identically for every question — three turns. Before: “how confident are you that you will get this right?” One line, no reasoning — a prior, before any work happens. Then the attempt — full reasoning allowed here, and only here. After: “how confident are you that your answer is correct?” One line again. Prospective versus retrospective matters: the second reading happens after the model has seen its own work.

Each question is sampled five times. That becomes important later — the repeats are what let us hold difficulty fixed and compare good and bad attempts at the very same question.

[→] And the load-bearing detail — the lock. The external grader scores every attempt and never speaks: no “correct,” no “wrong,” ever enters the conversation. So when confidence sags after a failed attempt, that isn't feedback. It came from inside.

Result 1 · evidence for finding 1 — it knows

The internal signal reads correctness better than the model reports it

How well each channel separates right from wrong — internal probe vs. stated confidence.

AUROC = Pr( score✓ > score✗ ) + ½ · Pr( tie ) pick one right + one wrong answer at random — how often does the reader rank the right one higher? · 0.50 = coin flip · 1.00 = perfect

The probe wins 12 of 16 cells (one tie) — biggest on math (+0.20), slim on geometry (+0.02).

Sixteen cells: model by subject. The grey bar is what the model says — its stated confidence. The coral bar is what a probe finds it knows. First, the ruler — point at the equation: pick one correct attempt and one incorrect attempt at random, and ask how often the reader ranks the correct one higher; half credit for ties. Point-five is a coin flip, one is perfect.

Two properties make this the right ruler here. It uses only ranks, so a model that says ninety-five for everything is scored fairly rather than punished for its scale — and ties get half credit precisely because stated confidence bunches hard at ninety-five. And it's robust to class imbalance, which matters because some cells run near ninety percent one class, where raw accuracy would be meaningless. Error bars throughout are bootstrap resamples.

Now the picture: coral above grey nearly everywhere, and the math cells in the middle are the strongest — up to point-nine-five internally while the stated number barely beats chance.

[→] Precisely: the probe wins twelve of sixteen cells, one tie. Biggest gap on math — plus point-two-oh. Slimmest on geometry — plus point-oh-two. The exceptions, before anyone finds them for me: small-sample cells, two of them Gemma-4. The claim is twelve-of-sixteen and a decisive math gap — not a clean sweep.

Result 3 · evidence for finding 1 — not just difficulty

It's judging this attempt — not "hard question"

Only questions it sometimes gets right, sometimes wrong across the 5 repeats. AUROC is computed within each such question, then averaged — so difficulty is constant by construction.

Not "this topic looks hard" — it tracks this particular attempt. (GLM·MATH: probe 0.89 · its own bet 0.48.)

The best objection in the room right now: “maybe the probe just learned which questions are hard — that's decodable too, and it isn't self-monitoring.” Fair — so we test it directly. Keep only the questions where the same model, across its five sampled attempts, sometimes succeeded and sometimes failed. Compute AUROC within each such question — difficulty is constant inside a question by construction — then average across questions. Anything above chance here is per-attempt information, full stop. (One statistical care: the bootstrap for these numbers resamples questions, not records, to respect the grouping.)

Coral: the probe still separates good attempts from bad, cell after cell. Red: P(True) — the model's own explicit bet on the token “True,” the strongest output-based baseline from the calibration literature — collapses toward the coin flip.

[→] GLM on MATH: probe point-eight-nine, its own bet point-four-eight. Same question, same difficulty, different attempts — the stream knows which attempt worked. That's the study's central dissociation: output-based confidence tracks the question; the internal signal tracks the attempt.

[→] The prospective/retrospective split from the protocol pays off here too. Retrospective beats prospective everywhere — on geometry, POST 0.66–0.70 versus PRE 0.52–0.62 — seeing its own work genuinely helps. And the two live at different places in the network: PRE-confidence reads peak early-to-mid and largely reflect difficulty decodable from the prompt (Mistral's at layer 20, Gemma's as early as layer 2), while the POST signal is chance at layer 0 and peaks mid-late — the computed self-assessment. A probe trained on POST barely transfers to PRE (0.39–0.67), and residualizing POST on PRE still leaves 0.59–0.87: two different directions, difficulty versus "did this attempt work."

Result 4a · evidence for finding 2 — is the signal real?

A reader with no answer key finds it too

That kills "your probe just memorized the data" — and the signal sits in the model's speakable wiring.

Now the memorization objection gets its answer. The lens has never seen a grade: its direction comes from the model's own output geometry, estimated on generic text. So we have two readers with no shared evidence — different information sources, different failure modes.

On Mistral, they converge: the lens lands at point-seven-six to point-eight-two — probe territory — and beats what the model says out loud. When two independent instruments point at the same direction, the artifact explanations get very thin.

[→] And convergence buys a second, deeper claim. The lens reads specifically through the model's output machinery — the pathway that turns thoughts into words. If it can see the correctness signal, that signal is sitting in the speakable subspace. The model could say this out loud. It doesn't — which sets up the twist.

Result 4c · evidence for finding 2 — why it breaks

Why: the "average wiring" smears

Remember the lens's one averaged map? That's only faithful if the wiring varies little across inputs.

Dense attention — stable transport

Fixed weights, no routing → change propagates the same way → the average stays sharp; "correct" and "wrong" stay distinct.

MoE & Mamba — rewire per input

Re-route per input (experts / selective scan) → the average is a smear → structure washes out.

Through the smear, "correct" and "wrong" become the same vector — it literally can't tell them apart.

Why would architecture break a reader? Recall the fine print from the methods: the lens is one map, averaged over inputs. An average is only faithful when the thing being averaged doesn't vary. Dense attention has fixed weights and no routing decisions — attention patterns still vary by input, but how a change propagates to the output is stable enough that the average stays faithful: the map stays sharp, and the readout vectors for “correct” and “wrong” stay well separated, cosine similarity around point-four. MoE and Mamba re-route on every input — a different subset of experts, an input-dependent scan. Average a hundred different wirings and what you get is a smear.

[→] And through that smear, the two readout vectors we need collapse onto each other — cosine point-nine-six, effectively the same vector. The lens isn't failing to find the signal; the smear has erased the difference between the words it reads with. One caution for the write-up: this is our leading explanation, with a clear fingerprint — not yet a direct measurement. The experiment that turns it into one is on the last slide. (Plain language out loud; “Jacobian” stays in Q&A.)

Result 5 · evidence for finding 3 — can we use it?

Causal for what it says — not what it gets right

Amplify the model's own signal (Mistral × MATH): stated confidence on WRONG answers falls, correct holds, random nudge is flat.

✓ what it says — moves

Confidence on wrong answers 80→65→43 as the gain goes 1→2→4; calibration error 0.31→0.13. Random nudge: flat. Dampening → more overconfident.

✗ what it gets right — doesn't

Nudge "toward correct" while answering → accuracy never rises (preliminary: 0.38 vs 0.41 baseline, every dose).

A monitor the model reads — not a lever that drives the answer.

The finale — cause, held to the bar from the methods. Experiment one: amplify the model's own correctness signal at the read site while it states its confidence. On wrong answers, the stated number slides from about eighty down to forty-three as the gain goes one, two, four — a clean dose–response. On right answers it barely moves — ninety-seven down to eighty-nine at the top gain — so the intervention is specific to failures, not generic degradation. The random direction at matched strength: flat. Dampening instead of amplifying makes the model more overconfident — the effect flips with the sign, the third control. And the headline calibration number: expected calibration error — the average gap between claimed confidence and realized accuracy — drops from point-three-one to point-one-three. Overconfidence roughly halved, by turning up a signal the model already had.

[→] Experiment two flips the question: inject “toward correct” while it's answering, and try to make it right. Nothing — point-three-eight versus a point-four-one baseline, at every dose we tried.

[→] So: a gauge, not a wheel. The direction carries the model's assessment of its own work; it is not a control line that produces better work. Which is exactly what you'd want from a monitor — and it's readable.
