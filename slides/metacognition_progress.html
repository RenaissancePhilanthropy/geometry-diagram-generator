[→] The perimeter is easy: three plus four, doubled. Fourteen. Correct. [→] The octagon? Twenty-eight is what you get when you count every line between two corners — including the eight sides. The real answer is twenty. A classic trap — and the model fell in with perfect poise.

[→] And that's the phenomenon: nothing on the outside separated the right answer from the wrong one. The wrong one ships, because nobody double-checks a ninety-five. The thing we're missing is a calibrated “I'm not sure” — a free safety margin that nobody is collecting.

The real question

Is it bluffing — or clueless?

Two completely different things can produce that same confident, wrong answer:

output → "…the answer is 28." · Confidence: 95

↓  same output — but what's going on inside?

Possibility 1

The bluffer

Somewhere inside, it registered that it's on thin ice — the doubt just never reached the words.

Possibility 2

The clueless one

Nothing inside flagged a problem. It's wrong and genuinely has no idea.

Does it know ≠ does it say. That gap is the whole talk.

So which is it? When a model does that — is it bluffing? Did something inside whisper “thin ice” and never make it to the words? Or is it clueless — silence all the way down?

Same output. Same ninety-five. But everything depends on the difference — because a bluffer is sitting on knowledge we could use, and a clueless model has nothing to give.

[→] So we split the question in two: does it know, and does it say? The space between those answers has a name — the knowing–saying gap — and it is the whole talk.

Mech-interp 101 · 1/2

How a model thinks — all you need

Text → tokens → a vector each (~5,000 numbers). Then every layer does one thing: read the running vector, add its result back.

That running total is the residual stream — the model's working memory. That's what we read.

And ideas live in it as directions — including one that tracks "I am about to be wrong."

A quick tour of the machine — genuinely all you need. Text becomes tokens. Each token becomes a vector, about five thousand numbers. And then one move, repeated forty to sixty times: read the running vector, compute something, add your contribution back. Nothing is ever overwritten. Every layer just adds.

[→] That accumulating sum has a name: the residual stream — each layer literally adds its residual on top. Think of it as the model's working memory, because it's the thing we'll be reading for the next twenty minutes.

[→] And the fact that makes this whole field possible: ideas live in that stream as directions. There's a direction for “this is a midpoint.” And there is one that tracks “I am about to be wrong.” (Dot products on request — Q&A.)

Mech-interp 101 · 2/2

Read, then poke

Probe (read, supervised)

Train a classifier on stored thoughts + right/wrong labels.

Lens (read, zero-shot)

Translate a mid-thought into words — no labels ever.

Steering (poke)

Add a direction while it generates; watch what changes.

Decodable ≠ used — so we use all three. A closer look at each, then the results.

Three tools. That's the entire kit. The probe reads with the answer key: train on graded snapshots, find the direction. Powerful — suspiciously powerful, a skeptic would say. The lens reads with no key at all: it turns a mid-stream thought into words using the model's own machinery. And steering doesn't read — it writes: inject a direction mid-thought and watch what changes.

[→] One rule keeps us honest all day: decodable is not the same as used. Reading proves a signal exists. Only writing proves it matters. Next three slides — how each one actually works.

Method · the probe, up close

Learning the "am I right?" direction

no leakage

Train/test split by question — scored strictly out-of-fold.

no cherry-picking

Reported at one fixed depth (~0.7 through) — never the best layer.

no surface cues

Identical wording every item; layer 0 decodes at chance.

Powerful — but it has seen the answer key. So every probe claim gets an honest second witness — next slide.

Here's the workhorse. Step one: thousands of snapshots of the residual stream — one per attempt, taken at the confidence token — each stamped with the external verdict, right or wrong. Step two: hand them to the simplest learner imaginable, a linear classifier, and ask for the one direction that separates the ✓ pile from the ✗ pile. Linear is the point — there's no room to hide clever circuitry. If it works, the information was just sitting there in the stream. Step three: score attempts it has never seen. One dot product.

And because trained readers deserve suspicion, three guardrails: train and test split by question, scored strictly out-of-fold. One fixed depth, about seventy percent through — never the flattering best layer. And identical wording everywhere, with layer zero decoding at chance.

[→] Even so — it saw the answer key. A skeptic can still say “memorized.” Which is why every probe claim in this talk gets a second witness who never did. Next slide.

Method · the lens, up close

Reading a thought with no answer key

No correctness label ever enters — the direction comes from the model's own wiring. An honest second witness for the trained probe.

Meet the witness with no answer key. Step one: a raw mid-stream thought — not words yet. Step two: push it through what I call the model's phrasebook — one map from thought-space to word-space, built once, by asking the model: averaged over ordinary text, how does a change here move the words you choose? And notice the fine print: one map. Averaged. Reused for everything. Tuck that away — it becomes the twist of this whole talk.

Step three: out come leanings over words. We check exactly two families — right, true, correct versus wrong, false, error — and the score is the lean.

[→] No label ever touches this pipeline. So when the lens agrees with the probe, memorization is off the table. (Its internal name is the Jacobian lens — keep that word for Q&A.)

Method · steering, up close

From reading to cause: nudge the thought

control 1 · dose

Sweep α gently → hard. Effects should grow with the dose.

control 2 · random

Random direction, same strength → must do nothing.

control 3 · reverse

Flip the sign → effect should flip too.

Reading shows correlation. If the targeted nudge moves behavior — and the random one doesn't — the model uses that direction. That's cause.

Reading is done; now we poke. While the model is generating — mid-thought, token by token — we reach into the stream and add alpha times our direction. Alpha is a dose dial. Then we watch downstream: does the stated confidence move? Does the accuracy move?

The discipline is the three controls. Real effects should grow with the dose. A random direction at the same strength must do nothing. Flip the sign, and the effect should flip.

[→] That's the difference between correlation and cause. When we get to the final result, hold it to exactly this bar.

Testbed 1 · geometry

A compiler as the judge

"an isosceles triangle with an inscribed circle..."
        ↓  model writes a RecipeDSL JSON program
parse → validate → lower → compile (SymPy) → geometric checks

Compiles + passes every invariant, or fails at a specific stage. Exact — no human judge, no LLM judge.

And hard on purpose: models pass only 13–39%. Metacognition is failure-hungry.

You cannot study “knows it's wrong” without an ironclad “wrong.” So testbed one grades itself: the model writes a small geometry program, and a symbolic compiler checks every promised property — tangency, perpendicularity, all of it. It compiles and passes everything, or it fails at a named stage. No human judge. No LLM judge. No vibes.

[→] And it's hard on purpose — pass rates between thirteen and thirty-nine percent. That's the design, not a flaw: metacognition research is failure-hungry. A model that's never wrong can't show you whether it knows when it is.

The experiment

Three turns, and the grader never tells

① Pre-task

"How confident that you will get this right?" — one line, no reasoning.

→
② Attempt

The model solves it. Reasoning allowed here — and only here.

→
③ Post-task

"How confident that your answer is correct?" — one line.

🔒 External grader scores the attempt — and never tells the model. So any confidence drop after a failure is blind self-assessment, not feedback.

The ritual, for every single question — three turns. Before: “how confident are you that you will get this right?” One line, no reasoning allowed. Then the attempt — think as long as you like here, and only here. After: “how confident are you that your answer is correct?” One line again.

[→] And the load-bearing detail — look at the lock. The grader never speaks. No “correct,” no “wrong,” ever enters the conversation. So when the model's confidence sags after a failed attempt, that isn't feedback. That came from inside.

Result 1 · the core finding

The internal signal reads correctness better than the model reports it

How well each channel separates right from wrong — internal probe vs. stated confidence.

AUROC = Pr( score✓ > score✗ ) + ½ · Pr( tie ) pick one right + one wrong answer at random — how often does the reader rank the right one higher? · 0.50 = coin flip · 1.00 = perfect

The probe wins 12 of 16 cells (one tie) — biggest on math (+0.20), slim on geometry (+0.02).

Sixteen cells. Five models, four subjects. The grey bar is what the model says; the coral bar is what a probe finds it knows. First, the ruler — point at the equation: grab one right answer and one wrong one at random, and ask how often the reader ranks the right one higher. Half credit for ties — and ties matter, because stated confidence bunches at ninety-five. Point-five is a coin flip. One is perfect.

Now look. Coral wins nearly everywhere — and the math cells in the middle are the showstopper: up to point-nine-five on the inside, while the mouth says barely better than chance.

[→] Precisely: the probe wins twelve of sixteen, one tie. Biggest gap on math — plus point-two-oh. Slimmest on geometry. And the three exceptions, before anyone finds them for me: small samples, two of them Gemma-4. The claim is twelve-of-sixteen and a decisive math gap — not a clean sweep.

Result 4 · why it breaks

Why: the "average wiring" smears

Remember the lens's one averaged map? That's only faithful if the wiring is the same every input.

Dense attention — one wiring

Same wiring every input → the average stays sharp → "correct" and "wrong" stay distinct.

MoE & Mamba — rewire per input

Re-route per input (experts / selective scan) → the average is a smear → structure washes out.

Through the smear, "correct" and "wrong" become the same vector — it literally can't tell them apart.

So why would architecture break a reader? Remember the phrasebook's fine print — one map, averaged over inputs. Averaging is only safe when the thing you're averaging doesn't change. Dense attention has one fixed wiring — and the average of one thing is itself. The map stays sharp, and “correct” and “wrong” sit far apart: cosine around point-four. MoE and Mamba re-route on every input — different experts, an input-dependent scan. Average a hundred different wirings and what you get is a smear.

[→] And through a smear, the two words we need collapse into the same vector — cosine point-nine-six. The lens isn't missing the signal. The smear erased the difference between “correct” and “wrong” themselves. (Plain language out loud; “Jacobian” stays in Q&A.)

Result 5 · can we use it?

Causal for what it says — not what it gets right

Amplify the model's own signal (Mistral × MATH): stated confidence on WRONG answers falls, correct holds, random nudge is flat.

✓ what it says — moves

Overconfidence on wrong answers 80→43. Random nudge: nothing.

✗ what it gets right — doesn't

Nudge "toward correct" while answering → accuracy never rises.

A monitor the model reads — not a lever that drives the answer.

The finale — cause. Experiment one: amplify the model's own signal at the read site, and watch its mouth. On wrong answers, stated confidence slides from about eighty-seven down to forty-three as we raise the dose — the model confesses. On right answers, it barely moves. And the random direction? Flat line. Grows with dose, direction-specific, calibration error cut in half. Causal — for what it says.

[→] Then we flip the game: shove it “toward correct” while it's working, and try to make it right. Nothing. Point-three-eight versus point-four-one baseline, at every dose we tried.

[→] Gauge, not wheel. You can tap the gauge and get honesty. You cannot steer the car from the fuel needle.
