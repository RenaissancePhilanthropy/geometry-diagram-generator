From the workshop · how the diagrams get made

The AI doesn't draw.
It writes recipes.

AI can produce endless diagrams, just sometimes not correct ones.
Here's how we closed that gap.

step-wise geometry diagram generator solution

1.The problem: good diagrams don't scale

Geometry practice runs on diagrams. Every problem needs an accurate figure, every skill needs many problems, and every student ideally gets fresh ones. Yet those figures are still made the way they were fifty years ago: an expert hand-places every point and double-checks every angle. It's careful, slow work, so good practice material is scarce and expensive, and the students who need it most have the least of it.

The obvious answer is generative AI, and it fails in an instructive way. Ask an image model for “a right triangle with a circle through all three corners” and you get something confident and wrong: a bent side, a circle that grazes two corners and ignores the third. Usually that's harmless. In a classroom it's disqualifying, because a geometry diagram is the one picture where almost right is worse than nothing. A student practicing on a “right angle” of 78° is being taught something false.

So the question isn't “can AI draw?” It's “can AI produce diagrams a teacher never has to proofread?” That's the bar we built this to clear.

2.Our solution: ask the AI for the recipe, not the picture

Think about giving someone directions to your house. You wouldn't paint the route from memory. You'd write instructions: left at the bakery, second right, gray door. Short, checkable, and anyone reliable can follow them exactly.

That's our whole approach. The AI never touches a pixel or eyeballs an angle. It may anchor a figure (corner B at the origin, say), but every relationship arrives as a step in a construction recipe, the same step-by-step build a student would do with compass and straightedge. “Make a triangle with a right angle here. Mark the midpoint of that side. Draw the circle centered there.”

That circle hitting all three corners isn't luck; it's a 2,300-year-old theorem (Thales'). The recipe made it inevitable, and the checks confirmed it anyway.

3.The math does the measuring, not the AI

A recipe still has to be carried out. We hand it to a computer algebra system: software that does geometry by calculation and has zero imagination. Given “the midpoint of A and C,” it doesn't eyeball the middle. It computes it: exactly, as a fraction, for a construction like this one; numerically, to strict tolerances, for harder ones.

This division of labor is the load-bearing wall:

The AI supplies intent. The mathematics supplies precision. Neither is asked to do the other's job.

The AI may pin an anchor point or two; every coordinate built on them comes from the math engine, not the AI. That division matters, because an AI asked to place points itself behaves like a person sketching from memory, and the result is plausible, confident, and slightly wrong:

4.Every diagram is graded before a student ever sees it

This is the part that turns “AI-generated” into “trustworthy.” A geometry question does more than describe a picture. It makes promises: “M is the midpoint.” “The lines are parallel.” “The angle is 90°.” Each promise is something software can measure.

So every promise encoded in the recipe becomes a numeric check, run against the computed geometry before the diagram ships. We call it what your teacher called it: the red pen. A failed check sends the homework back to the AI with notes (redo it, here's what was wrong). The AI gets three attempts; if the checks still fail, nothing ships. No diagram reaches a student on “looks fine to us.”

6.Under the hood: one diagram, step by step

For the technically curious, here is the exact journey of the diagram from Try it №1, with the real artifacts at each stage. (The recipe you played there was simplified for display; below is the real thing.)

Step 1 · The ask. Plain language in:

Draw triangle ABC with a right angle at B. M is the midpoint
of AC. Draw the circle centered at M through A.

Step 2 · The recipe becomes structured data. The AI's recipe is lowered into an intermediate representation: a list of typed construction steps, plus the promises to verify. This is the AI's entire contribution. Notice it never states where M is; it only says how M is defined.

{"id":"B", "kind":"point_fixed", "x":0, "y":0}
{"id":"A", "kind":"point_fixed", "x":0, "y":3}
{"id":"C", "kind":"point_fixed", "x":4, "y":0}
{"id":"T", "kind":"triangle", "a":"A", "b":"B", "c":"C"}
{"id":"M", "kind":"point_midpoint", "p":"A", "q":"C"}
{"id":"k", "kind":"circle_center_point", "center":"M", "through":"A"}
{"id":"MA", "kind":"segment", "a":"M", "b":"A"}
{"id":"MB", "kind":"segment", "a":"M", "b":"B"}
{"id":"MC", "kind":"segment", "a":"M", "b":"C"}

checks:
{"kind":"right_angle", "angle":{"a":"A", "o":"B", "b":"C"}}
{"kind":"equal_length", "segs":["MA","MB","MC"]}

Step 3 · SymPy computes the exact geometry. Each definition compiles, in dependency order, into an object in SymPy, an open-source computer algebra library. Look closely at the arithmetic: M lands at (2, 3/2) and the radius is exactly 5/2. Fractions, not rounded decimals. A construction this simple stays exact end to end; constructions that need numeric solving are computed to high precision and then verified. Either way, nothing here was guessed.

A = Point2D(0, 3)    B = Point2D(0, 0)    C = Point2D(4, 0)
M = Point2D(2, 3/2)               # the midpoint, computed
k = Circle(Point2D(2, 3/2), 5/2)  # radius = |MA|, exact

Step 4 · The red pen runs. Every promise becomes a numeric test against those objects:

✓ right_angle   angle A-B-C measures 90°
✓ equal_length  |MA| = |MB| = |MC| = 5/2

Each test must pass within a strict numeric tolerance. Any failure rejects the diagram and returns the failing checks to the AI as revision notes, for at most three attempts; after that the system gives up rather than ship.

Step 5 · Only now is anything drawn. SymPy's coordinates are translated into TikZ, a LaTeX drawing language, and a LaTeX engine renders the final SVG. The drawing layer never invents a coordinate; it inherits verified ones.

\tkzDefPoint(0,3){A}   \tkzDefPoint(0,0){B}
\tkzDefPoint(4,0){C}   \tkzDefPoint(2,1.5){M}
\tkzDrawPolygon(A,B,C)
\tkzDrawCircle(M,A)

7.The impact: what verified generation makes possible

It changes what you can trust. Every diagram from this pipeline arrives with a receipt: the promises it made and the measurements proving it kept them. It can go in front of a student without a manual geometry check, because that check already ran, something hand-made materials can't scale and unverified AI can't offer.

It changes the economics of practice. When every diagram grades itself, a verified figure costs machine-seconds instead of expert-minutes. A full item bank for a course, with a fresh variant for every student, can be built in an afternoon, leaving human judgment for the question software can't answer: is this problem worth asking?

And it gives the field a yardstick. We released GeoGenBench, a public benchmark of 801 diagram-generation problems (600 procedurally generated, 201 extracted from K-12 textbooks) where a correct diagram's properties are machine-checkable, so anyone can measure, rather than eyeball, how well an AI draws.

The principle travels beyond geometry:

Let the AI propose; let the math verify. Generative models are marvelous at intent and unreliable at precision, so route precision through machinery that can't fudge, and check every promise before anyone learns from it.

That's the generation component: the AI writes the recipe, the mathematics follows it exactly, and the red pen grades everything, every time, for every student.
