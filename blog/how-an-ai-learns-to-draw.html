From the workshop · how the diagrams get made

The AI doesn't draw.
It writes recipes.

How we get trustworthy geometry diagrams out of a language model — by never letting it guess where anything goes.

geometry diagram generator · a plain-language tour of the generation pipeline

Ask an image-generating AI for “a right triangle with a circle passing through all three corners” and you'll get something that looks confident and is wrong: a triangle with a suspiciously bent side, a circle that grazes two corners and politely ignores the third, a stray label floating in space.

For most uses of AI images, that's fine — nobody checks the shadows in a fantasy landscape. But we make practice materials for learning math, and a geometry diagram is the one kind of picture where almost right is worse than nothing. A student who practices on a diagram whose “right angle” is 78° isn't just unhelped. They're mis-taught.

So we built a system where the AI never touches a pixel, never places a point, and never estimates an angle. Here's the trick.

1.Don't ask for the picture. Ask for the recipe.

Think about how you'd give someone directions to your house. You wouldn't paint them a picture of the route from memory — you'd write instructions: left at the bakery, second right, gray door. The instructions are short, checkable, and someone (or something) reliable can follow them exactly.

That's what our language model produces: not a drawing, but a construction recipe — the same kind of step-by-step build you'd do with a compass and straightedge in geometry class. “Make a triangle with a right angle here. Mark the midpoint of that side. Draw the circle centered there, through this corner.”

That circle hitting all three corners isn't luck — it's a 2,300-year-old theorem (Thales'). The recipe made it inevitable, and the checks confirmed it anyway.

2.The math does the measuring

A recipe still has to be carried out. We hand it to a computer algebra system — software that does geometry the way your most pedantic classmate does: with exact arithmetic and zero imagination. Given “the midpoint of A and C,” it doesn't eyeball the middle. It computes it, to as many digits as you like.

This is the load-bearing division of labor in the whole system:

The language model supplies intent. The algebra system supplies precision. Neither is asked to do the other's job.

Every coordinate in the final diagram comes out of the math engine, never out of the AI. Which matters, because language models asked to place points directly do what a person sketching from memory does — they get it plausibly, confidently, slightly wrong:

3.Every diagram gets graded before you see it

Here's our favorite part. A geometry question doesn't just describe a picture — it makes promises. “M is the midpoint.” “The lines are parallel.” “The angle is 90°.” Each promise is something you can measure.

So the system extracts every promise from the recipe and verifies it against the computed geometry, numerically, before the diagram ships. We call it what your teacher called it: the red pen. If any check fails, the diagram is rejected and the AI gets its homework back — redo it, here's what was wrong — until every promise holds or we give up honestly.

5.Why go to all this trouble?

Because it changes what you're allowed to trust. A diagram from this pipeline isn't “AI-generated, looks good to us” — it arrives with a receipt: the list of promises it made and the measurements proving it kept them. That's a different category of artifact. It can go in front of a student unsupervised.

It also changes what you can build. If every diagram grades itself, you can generate thousands — a practice set for every skill, a fresh variant for every student, an entire test bank — and machine-check the lot, reserving human eyes for the judgment calls software can't make. (We also released a public benchmark built on this idea: 801 geometry prompts where the properties of a correct diagram are machine-checkable, so anyone can measure how well any AI system draws.)

The deeper principle travels beyond geometry, and it's the one we'd tattoo on the project if projects had arms:

Let the AI propose; let the math verify. Generative models are marvelous at intent and unreliable at precision — so route the precision through machinery that cannot fudge, and check every promise before anyone learns from it.

That's the generation component. The AI writes the recipe, the algebra follows it exactly, and the red pen grades everything — every single time.
