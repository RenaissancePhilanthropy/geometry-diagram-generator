1.The problem: close is not correct

Math class runs on pictures. Every geometry problem needs one. And every student needs lots of problems to practice on.

But those pictures are still drawn by hand. A person places every point. A person checks every angle. That is slow, careful work. So good practice problems are rare.

Why not just ask an AI to draw one? Try it. Ask for “a right triangle with a circle through all three corners.” You get something that looks close. But look again. One side is bent. The circle misses a corner.

In art class, close is fine. In math class, close is wrong. A kid who practices on a “right angle” of 78° is learning something false.

2.Our fix: ask for the recipe, not the picture

Think about giving a friend directions to your house. You would not paint a picture of the whole trip. You would write steps. Turn left at the bakery. Take the second right. Look for the gray door.

Steps are short. Steps are easy to check. And anyone careful can follow them exactly.

That is our whole trick. The AI never draws. It writes a construction recipe, the same kind of step-by-step build you would do with a compass and ruler. Make a triangle with a square corner at B. Find M, the exact middle of side AC. Draw a circle around M that touches A.

The circle touches all three corners. That is not luck. A 2,300 year old math rule (Thales' theorem) says it must happen. The recipe made it certain. The checks proved it anyway.

So three workers share the job. Each one does what it is best at, and nothing else:

The AI plans

It writes the recipe. It says what to build. It never says where the dots go.

The math builds

It follows the steps exactly. It computes every point. It never guesses.

The red pen checks

It measures every promise. One failed check, and nobody sees the picture.

The AI supplies the plan. The math supplies the precision. Neither one is asked to do the other's job.

3.Every picture is graded first

A geometry problem makes promises. M is the middle. The corner is 90°. The circle touches all three corners.

Here is the key: a computer can measure every one of those promises.

So before any picture ships, we measure them all. We call it what your teacher called it: the red pen. If a check fails, the picture is rejected. The recipe goes back to the AI with a note: here is what was wrong, try again. The AI gets three tries. If the checks still fail, nothing ships at all.

4.The whole assembly line

One request rides through five stations. A failed check gets sent back, like a returned draft. Tap each station:

↺ fail a check? the work goes back to station 2 with notes

A peek under the hood for curious readers: the real files behind Try it №2

Step 1 · The ask. Plain words go in:

Draw triangle ABC with a right angle at B. M is the midpoint
of AC. Draw the circle centered at M through A.

Step 2 · The recipe becomes data a computer can read. Every step gets a type. Every promise gets listed. This is the AI's whole job. Notice: it never says where M is. It only says what M means.

{"id":"B", "kind":"point_fixed", "x":0, "y":0}
{"id":"A", "kind":"point_fixed", "x":0, "y":3}
{"id":"C", "kind":"point_fixed", "x":4, "y":0}
{"id":"T", "kind":"triangle", "a":"A", "b":"B", "c":"C"}
{"id":"M", "kind":"point_midpoint", "p":"A", "q":"C"}
{"id":"k", "kind":"circle_center_point", "center":"M", "through":"A"}
{"id":"MA", "kind":"segment", "a":"M", "b":"A"}
{"id":"MB", "kind":"segment", "a":"M", "b":"B"}
{"id":"MC", "kind":"segment", "a":"M", "b":"C"}

checks:
{"kind":"right_angle", "angle":{"a":"A", "o":"B", "b":"C"}}
{"kind":"equal_length", "segs":["MA","MB","MC"]}

Step 3 · The math engine computes the exact points. We use SymPy, a free math library. Look at the numbers: M lands at (2, 3/2) and the radius is exactly 5/2. Fractions, not rounded guesses. Harder builds use high-precision numbers, then get verified. Either way, nothing is eyeballed.

A = Point2D(0, 3)    B = Point2D(0, 0)    C = Point2D(4, 0)
M = Point2D(2, 3/2)               # the midpoint, computed
k = Circle(Point2D(2, 3/2), 5/2)  # radius = |MA|, exact

Step 4 · The red pen runs. Every promise becomes a test:

✓ right_angle   angle A-B-C measures 90°
✓ equal_length  |MA| = |MB| = |MC| = 5/2

Each test must pass within a very tight margin. Any failure rejects the picture and sends the notes back to the AI. Three failed tries, and the system gives up rather than ship.

Step 5 · Only now is anything drawn. The verified points go to TikZ, a drawing language, which renders the final image. The drawing layer never invents a point. It inherits checked ones.

\tkzDefPoint(0,3){A}   \tkzDefPoint(0,0){B}
\tkzDefPoint(4,0){C}   \tkzDefPoint(2,1.5){M}
\tkzDrawPolygon(A,B,C)
\tkzDrawCircle(M,A)

You can trust the pictures. Every diagram comes with a receipt: the promises it made, and the measurements that prove it kept them. A teacher never has to proofread it. The proofreading already ran.

Practice gets cheap. A checked picture used to take expert minutes. Now it takes machine seconds. A whole course of fresh problems, with a different version for every student, can be built in an afternoon.

And anyone can keep score. Our public test, GeoGenBench, has 801 diagram problems (600 built from templates, 201 taken from real K-12 textbooks). Each one can be checked by a machine. So anyone can measure how well an AI draws, instead of eyeballing it.

The big idea travels beyond geometry:

Let the AI plan. Let the math measure. Check every promise before a student sees it.

That is the whole story. The AI writes the recipe. The math follows it exactly. And the red pen grades everything, every time, for every student.
